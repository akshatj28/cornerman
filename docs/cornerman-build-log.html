The loop

What happens after a workout

You finish lifting and hit share in Hevy. Two taps later the session is on a server you own, parsed into sets and reps, and measured against your goal. Solid lines are built and running. Dashed lines are next.

The green path is live today. Everything amber is the next build.

Reconnaissance

Eight routes to the same data

Getting workouts out of a fitness app turned out to be the entire difficulty of this project. The analysis engine took an afternoon. The data path took days. Most of the value here is the negative results — the routes that don't work, and why.

Decisions

What we chose, and what it cost

Every one of these was a real fork with a real downside. Naming the cost matters more than defending the choice.

Where the data lives

Our own SQLite, not a fitness platform

One file on one server, holding every workout, keyed so the same session can never be counted twice.

The cost

No social graph, no free mobile app to view it in, and we maintain the schema ourselves.

Gave up: convenience. Kept: the data can't be paywalled or re-licensed out from under us.
Who does the arithmetic

Plain code computes, the AI only narrates

Estimated max, trend, zone splits, goal gap — all deterministic Python. Same input, same answer, forever.

The cost

Every new metric has to be written by hand rather than asked for in a sentence.

Gave up: flexibility. Kept: numbers you can audit — and a free model stays sufficient, because it never has to calculate.
How workouts arrive

Two taps, forever

Share from Hevy, tap the shortcut, done. No polling, no tokens, no expiry.

The cost

It isn't invisible. Forget to share and the coach doesn't know you trained.

Gave up: magic. Kept: ₹0, and nothing that breaks next month.
How it talks to you

Email, not WhatsApp

Reports go to an inbox. Replying to one is how you tell it how the session felt.

The cost

Less immediate than a phone notification, and easier to leave unread.

Gave up: immediacy. Kept: free outbound messages, and a threaded reply that carries more than a chat line.
Where it runs

A free cloud machine, not a laptop at home

An always-free VM, awake around the clock, so nothing depends on a laptop being open.

The cost

A signup with a card check, a region locked at creation, and someone else's free tier to depend on.

Gave up: full independence. Kept: a coach that's awake when you train at 6am.
Proof

The engine, checked against real lifts

Nine months of training loaded into the database, then read back by the analysis engine. These matched what we'd computed independently from the raw export — which is how we know the whole chain is sound.

LiftEstimated maxSessions usedWhat the engine saw
Squat (Barbell) 110.0 kg 13 of 15 Climbing overall, flat for the last few weeks
Deadlift (Trap bar) 150.0 kg 7 Goal already met — holding, not climbing
Bench Press (Barbell) 81.7 kg 10 Last session in April — pressing moved to dumbbells
DETAIL

Why the squat used 13 sessions, not 15

Two early sessions were light technique days that a naive trend line would read as real maxes, dragging the slope upward. The engine drops them by comparing each session against the median of your own history for that lift — not a hardcoded threshold — so it adapts per exercise and per person.

DETAIL

Why a bench number from April is correct

Barbell pressing stopped in April; incline dumbbell took over and is now the most-logged lift in the database. The engine doesn't paper over that. A goal set on "bench" has to say which bench — and that ambiguity is exactly the kind of thing the coach should raise rather than guess at.

Transferable

What this taught us

Most of these would apply to any project that has to get data out of somebody else's app.

01

The analysis was the easy part

The whole scoring engine — estimated maxes, trends, outlier rejection, goal projection — came together in an afternoon and was right the first time. Getting the data to feed it took days. Budget accordingly: the interesting work is rarely where the time goes.

02

Prove the valuable half with the cheap path first

A plain CSV export validated the entire analysis engine against real training before a single live API was touched. When the live routes then failed, nothing was wasted — the thing that mattered was already known to work.

03

Attempts are a budget, not a free action

Login endpoints are rate-limited. Probing one "just to see" costs an attempt, and enough of them locks you out and poisons every reading afterward. Reconnaissance means spending one careful attempt when the meter is clean, not ten quick ones.

04

Manual beats fragile

A two-tap habit that works forever is worth more than an automated pipeline that needs re-authenticating every month and breaks whenever the vendor ships an update. Automation is only a win if it stays automated.

05

Keeping the model out of arithmetic keeps it cheap

Because the AI never has to calculate — only interpret numbers already computed — a small free model is enough for the job. The architectural discipline and the running cost turn out to be the same decision.

06

Build the fragile plumbing where it will actually run

Reverse-engineered clients can't be written blind. You need to make a real call, see the real response, and shape the parser to it. Anything written without that loop is guesswork that has to be redone.

07

Terminals scramble large pastes

Pasting a long file into an SSH session silently collided lines and swallowed terminators. Writing files in twenty-line chunks, checking the tail after each, turned a mystifying failure into a mechanical one. Small verifiable steps beat one big invisible one.

08

Negative results are the deliverable

Five blocked routes look like failure in a progress report and read like gold to the next person attempting this. Documenting exactly which doors are shut, and what's behind each one, was the most reusable output of the whole build.
