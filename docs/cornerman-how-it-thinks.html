Cornerman · how the coach works

The coach can look things up.
Deciding how it looks them up
changed everything.

Cornerman is an AI coach that reads your training log and talks to you about it. This explains what it can actually do, and the one design decision that turned out to matter far more than expected — not what the coach knows, but how it goes and gets it.

Three things the coach can do

Before the interesting part, here is what was built. None of this is the AI "just knowing" things — each is a specific ability it was given, and each one was tested against the real training database.

Look things up

Read the log

It can search the training history itself — not a summary someone prepared for it. Ask something nobody anticipated and it goes and finds out.

Asked for the most-logged exercise, it searched and answered: Kettlebell Swing, 80 sets.
Remember

Keep notes

It has its own notebook, separate from the training data. Goals, injuries, things you have told it before. Written by the coach, read back later.

Told once that barbell bench stopped in April. Days later, in a fresh conversation, it brought that up unprompted.
Compute exactly

Use the calculator

For the numbers that matter — estimated maximums, progress rates, whether a goal is on track — there is a verified calculator it can call instead of working it out itself.

Squat estimated max: 110.0 kg. Same answer every time it is asked.

The question nobody expects to matter

Imagine the coach sitting at a desk. Your entire training history is in a filing cabinet across the room. You ask a question. How should the coach answer it?

There are two obvious approaches, and they sound almost identical. In practice they behave completely differently — different costs, different mistakes, different strengths.

APPROACH ONE

Walk to the cabinet

The coach fetches one thing, comes back, reads it, decides what it needs next, and walks over again. Repeat until it has enough to answer.

Every trip is a separate message to the AI service. A single question took around nine of them.

Strength

It always uses the verified calculator, so the numbers are exact.

Weakness

It has to guess what the folders are called. It looked for "Squat", found nothing, and concluded there were no squat sessions at all — when there are fifteen, filed under "Squat (Barbell)".

APPROACH TWO

Bring the whole cabinet

Everything arrives at once. Nine months of training, every set, every weight — handed over in a single message along with the question.

Your whole log is smaller than it sounds: about six thousand words. It fits easily.

Strength

One message instead of nine. And because it can see everything, it spots patterns — it noticed you had been stuck at the same weight since May, which the other approach never mentioned.

Weakness

With no calculator in reach, it does the maths in its head. It estimated your squat max at 115 kg. The verified answer is 110.

APPROACH THREE

Cabinet and calculator

The whole log arrives, and stapled to the front is a short sheet of numbers the verified calculator worked out beforehand — with an instruction: use these, do not redo them.

Still one message. Still sees everything.

Strength

Correct numbers and pattern-spotting together, at the cost of a single message.

Status

Built, not yet the default. The current coach uses approach two while we get the rest of the system running.

What actually happened when we tried it

The same question, asked three ways: "How is my squat progressing, and am I on track for 130 kg by December?" The verified answer for the current squat maximum is 110 kg.

Same question · different approaches
Walk to the cabinet

Failed. Searched for "Squat", found nothing under that exact name, and stopped looking.

"You have zero squat sessions logged in the database, so there's no trend to show."
Whole cabinet

Good advice, slightly wrong number. Said 115 kg instead of 110. But it saw the plateau and gave a concrete next step.

"Your top sets show 100 kg × 3–4 reps consistently since May — push the next block to 105 × 4–5 or 110 × 3."
The verified calculator

Exact, every time. No advice, no judgement — just the number the coaching is built on.

110.0 kg · 13 sessions used · rising 1.2 kg per week

The approach with perfect arithmetic gave the worst answer. The approach that guessed the number gave the most useful coaching.

That is the whole lesson, and it was not obvious in advance. Being able to look things up one at a time sounds more rigorous than being handed everything. In practice it made the coach short-sighted — it could only see what it thought to ask for, and it asked for the wrong thing.

The reason we switched: how often you're allowed to ask

The AI service is free, but free comes with a cap — not on how much you can say, on how many times a day you can speak. Roughly fifty messages a day on a free account.

That sounds like plenty until you remember approach one uses nine messages per question. The arithmetic is brutal.

~5 questions per day
walking to the cabinet
~50 questions per day
sending everything at once
6,000 words in your entire
training history

Five questions a day is not a coach. A morning note, a report after training, and two replies would use it up before lunch — and then it goes silent with no explanation. Sending everything at once costs one message and buys back the whole day.

The mistake we decided to live with

Accepted, knowingly

A five-kilo error, for now

Approach two says 115 kg when the truth is 110. That is not nothing — it makes the gap to a 130 kg goal look fifteen kilos instead of twenty, which flatters the picture.

We are keeping it anyway for the first version, because the alternative approach failed the same question outright, and because a coach that answers fifty times a day with a small error is more useful right now than one that answers five times a day precisely. Approach three closes the gap when we switch to it.

This is written down deliberately. A known, measured, temporary flaw is a decision. The same flaw undocumented is just a bug nobody noticed.

What the coach is not allowed to do

It reads your training history and writes its own notes. Those are two different permissions, and the difference is enforced by the database itself rather than by asking the coach politely.

READ ONLY

It cannot change your training data. The connection it uses is opened in a mode where writing is impossible. Even a direct instruction to delete everything comes back refused by the database, not intercepted by a filter that might have a gap in it.

OWN NOTES

It can write to its own notebook. A separate place for goals, injuries and observations. This is how it remembers between conversations — and it is the only thing it can add to.

LOGGED

Every lookup it makes is recorded. When it searches the history, the search is printed. Nothing it does with your data is invisible to you.

The coach can read your training, remember what matters, and reason about both. What took the longest to work out was not teaching it any of that — it was deciding how much to hand it at once, and discovering that generosity beat precision.
